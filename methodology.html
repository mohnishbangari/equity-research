Methodology

A framework that can be checked.

The goal is not to make the most complicated model possible. It is to make the assumptions, evidence and limits clear enough that another person could reproduce the study.

1. Define the historical window

Start date: July 20, 2026. End date: September 25, 2026. Closing prices are used at both dates. The window is already finished, so there is no unfinished future performance in the project.

2. Build an equal-weight portfolio

The $100,000 model portfolio allocates $12,500 to each company. Fractional shares keep each starting weight exactly equal. Positions are held unchanged through the end date.

3. Research the business

Each company report starts with how the business makes money, then identifies company-specific KPIs, competitive advantages, growth drivers, risks and one-time items that can distort headline results.

4. Evaluate valuation separately

A strong business is not automatically an attractive stock at any price. Trailing and forward P/E are used as simple reference points, with adjustments where headline earnings are distorted by unusual items.

5. Compare with a benchmark

The S&P 500 price index is used as a simple benchmark. Because the individual-company calculation also uses raw closing prices, dividends are excluded from both sides of the comparison.

6. Review the outcome

The final step asks what the period actually showed. Price movement is compared with operating evidence, but the project does not claim that one earnings release or one thesis caused a stock's return.

Research design

Retrospective historical study.

The final company reports use information available through September 25, 2026. The historical portfolio is used to study how the selected companies performed during the fixed July 20 to September 25 period while their operating evidence evolved.

Known limitations

  • The study period is short and may be dominated by market sentiment.
  • The portfolio is concentrated in eight large U.S. companies.
  • Dividends, taxes, transaction costs and rebalancing are excluded.
  • P/E is not equally informative for every business model.
  • Company disclosures are primary sources but still reflect management's presentation of results.
  • Observed stock returns do not establish causation.
